William James was born in a hotel on Astor Place in New York, the eldest son of a wealthy, eccentric theologian who moved his five children restlessly between America and Europe in search of the perfect education. His younger brother was the novelist Henry James. It was said that William wrote psychology like a novelist, and Henry wrote novels like a psychologist.

He wanted to be a painter, then studied chemistry and medicine at Harvard, and went on a scientific expedition to Brazil. In his late twenties he fell into a deep depression, and he later said he pulled himself out of it by an act of will: deciding that his first act of free will would be to believe in free will.

He taught at Harvard for thirty-five years, first physiology, then psychology, then philosophy, and founded one of America's first psychology laboratories. His great book *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) took twelve years to write. In it he described thought not as a chain of separate ideas but as something that flows, and named it "the stream of consciousness." Novelists like Virginia Woolf and James Joyce later tried to put that stream on the page.

His later books are just as readable. *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) studies conversions, mystical visions and saints with respect and curiosity, as experiences, not doctrines. *Pragmatism* (1907) argued that the meaning of an idea lies in the difference it makes in practice. He died in New Hampshire in 1910, and his prose is still a pleasure: warm, funny and alive.
