Susan Rosenblatt was born in New York and grew up in Arizona and California; she took her stepfather's surname, Sontag. She was a fierce, early reader, finished high school at fifteen, and studied at Berkeley and then the University of Chicago, where at seventeen she married a young sociology lecturer, Philip Rieff, after knowing him for ten days. She went on to graduate work at Harvard and Oxford.

In the 1960s she became one of the most visible critics in America. Her essay "Notes on 'Camp'" (1964) took a sensibility of exaggeration and theatricality, associated with gay culture, seriously for the first time. "Against Interpretation" (1966) argued that critics spent too much time digging for hidden meanings and too little attending to the experience of art itself.

Her later books turned to how we look at the world. *On Photography* (1977) asked what happens to us when we see everything through cameras. Diagnosed with cancer in her forties, she wrote *Illness as Metaphor* (1978), against the myths that blame the sick for their illness, and later *AIDS and Its Metaphors*. *Regarding the Pain of Others* (2003) returned to images of war and suffering. She also wrote novels, including *The Volcano Lover*, and directed films.

She was a public intellectual of a kind that hardly exists anymore: she went to Sarajevo during the siege in the 1990s and staged *Waiting for Godot* there by candlelight. She died in New York in 2004. Her essays remain a model of how to think in public, seriously and with style.
