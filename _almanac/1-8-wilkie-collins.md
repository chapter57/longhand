William Wilkie Collins was born in London, the son of a successful landscape painter, and named after his godfather, the painter David Wilkie. He spent two of his boyhood years in Italy, tried and abandoned a career in the tea trade, trained as a lawyer, and never practiced. What he loved was a story.

In 1851 he met Charles Dickens, who became his closest friend. They acted in amateur plays together, traveled together, and wrote together, and Dickens published Collins's fiction in his magazines. It was there, in 1859 and 1860, that *The Woman in White* appeared in weekly parts. A young drawing teacher walking at night meets a mysterious woman dressed entirely in white, who has escaped from an asylum, and the novel unwinds a conspiracy of stolen identity, told in turn by its different characters, like witnesses in court. Readers were gripped. Shops sold Woman in White perfume and cloaks.

*The Moonstone* (1868) is about a great yellow diamond, stolen from a temple in India, that vanishes from a young woman's room in a Yorkshire country house on the night of her birthday. A brilliant, melancholy detective, Sergeant Cuff, who loves roses, comes to investigate. T. S. Eliot called it "the first, the longest, and the best of modern English detective novels."

Collins lived unconventionally. He never married, and kept two households with two women, Caroline Graves and Martha Rudd, with whom he had three children. He suffered terribly from gout and took large doses of laudanum, an opium tincture, which plays a part in *The Moonstone*. He died in London in 1889.
