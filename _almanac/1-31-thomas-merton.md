Thomas Merton was born in Prades, in the south of France, to a New Zealand painter father and an American mother. His mother died when he was six, his father when he was fifteen, and he grew up between France, England and New York. He went to Cambridge, where he drank and caroused and fathered a child, and was sent home in disgrace.

At Columbia University in New York he became a writer, a jazz lover and an editor of the college magazines, and surprised his friends by converting to Catholicism in 1938. In December 1941 he gave away his belongings and entered the Abbey of Our Lady of Gethsemani, a community of Trappist monks in the hills of Kentucky, who lived by rules of silence, prayer and manual labor. He took the name Father Louis.

His abbot asked him to write his life story. *The Seven Storey Mountain* (1948), its title taken from Dante's mountain of Purgatory, told of a young man's search through a worldly life to the monastery. It sold hundreds of thousands of copies and drew many young men to the monasteries after the war.

He went on to write some seventy books, of essays, poetry and spiritual writing, including *New Seeds of Contemplation*. In the 1960s he wrote against nuclear war, racism and the Vietnam War, and was drawn to Buddhism and to dialogue with thinkers of other faiths. He was allowed to live as a hermit in a small cinder-block house in the woods. In December 1968, while attending a conference of monks in Bangkok, he was killed by electric shock from a faulty fan. He was fifty-three.
