James Baldwin was born in Harlem in 1924, the eldest of nine children, and raised by his mother and a stern stepfather who was a preacher. At fourteen, Baldwin became a preacher himself, and for three years he held congregations spellbound. He later said that the pulpit taught him how to hold a room, and that he left it because he wanted to tell the truth in a different way.

He found that way through books. A teacher at his junior high school was the poet Countee Cullen, and an older friend, the painter Beauford Delaney, showed him that a Black man could live his life as an artist. In 1948, worn down by American racism, Baldwin bought a one-way ticket to Paris with about forty dollars to his name.

In France he finished his first novel, *Go Tell It on the Mountain* (1953), drawn from his Harlem boyhood and the storefront church of his youth. Two years later came *Notes of a Native Son*, essays so clear and unsparing that they changed what American essays could do. *Giovanni's Room* (1956), a love story between two men in Paris, was rejected by his publisher; he published it elsewhere.

Baldwin went home to take part in the civil rights movement. In 1962 *The New Yorker* devoted most of an issue to a long essay of his about race and religion in America, and the next year it appeared as *The Fire Next Time*, which became a bestseller. He was on the cover of *Time*. He traveled the South, spoke at rallies, and argued, always, that America could not be free until it faced the truth about itself.

He spent his last years in Saint-Paul-de-Vence, a hill town in the south of France, and died there of cancer on December 1, 1987. He left notes for an unfinished book about three murdered friends, Medgar Evers, Malcolm X and Martin Luther King Jr.; decades later it became the documentary *I Am Not Your Negro*. His sentences, long and rolling like sermons, are still quoted at every moment when America argues with itself.
