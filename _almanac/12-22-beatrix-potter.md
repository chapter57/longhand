Beatrix Potter was born in London in 1866 to a wealthy family and educated at home by governesses. She was a lonely child, and she filled her schoolroom with animals: rabbits, mice, a hedgehog, frogs, a bat. She drew them constantly, with great precision, and on family holidays in Scotland and the Lake District she drew plants and fungi, too.

As a young woman she became a serious amateur scientist, making hundreds of detailed watercolors of mushrooms and studying how their spores grew. In 1897 she wrote a paper on the subject, but women were not allowed to present at the Linnean Society, so her uncle had to submit it for her. It was never published.

In 1893, she wrote a letter to Noel Moore, the five-year-old son of her former governess, who was ill in bed. Having run out of news, she told him a story about four little rabbits, Flopsy, Mopsy, Cottontail and Peter, and drew pictures to go with it. Years later she borrowed the letter back and turned it into a little book. Six publishers turned it down. In 1901 she had 250 copies printed at her own expense. The next year the firm of Frederick Warne published it in color, and *The Tale of Peter Rabbit* sold tens of thousands of copies in its first year.

More books followed, small enough for a child's hands: Squirrel Nutkin, Mrs. Tiggy-Winkle, Jemima Puddle-Duck. She fell in love with her editor, Norman Warne, and they became engaged, but he died suddenly a few weeks later. With her royalties she bought Hill Top, a farm in the village of Near Sawrey in the Lake District, and slowly built a new life there.

She married a local solicitor, William Heelis, in 1913, and spent her later years as a farmer and sheep breeder, raising the hardy local Herdwick sheep and winning prizes for them. She bought farm after farm to protect the land from development. When she died at home on December 22, 1943, she left more than four thousand acres, including fourteen farms, to the National Trust, which still looks after them.
