Thomas Stearns Eliot was born in St. Louis in 1888, into a distinguished New England family, and studied philosophy at Harvard. In 1914 he went to England on a fellowship and stayed for the rest of his life. He married quickly and unhappily, and to support himself he taught school and then worked for eight years in the foreign department of Lloyds Bank in London.

He wrote some of the most important poetry and criticism of the century in his spare time. "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" (1915), about a timid man who never quite dares to speak, opened with an evening "spread out against the sky / Like a patient etherized upon a table," and modern poetry began to sound different. *The Waste Land* (1922), a broken, haunted poem about a civilization after the First World War, made of fragments of myth, scripture and overheard voices, was the most influential poem of its time.

His essays were just as influential. "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919) argued that a poet writes with the whole of literature behind him, and that every new work changes how we read the old ones. Other essays revived the reputations of the seventeenth-century poets John Donne and Andrew Marvell. For decades, students learned to read poetry through Eliot's eyes.

He became a publisher, at Faber & Faber, where he championed younger poets, joined the Church of England, and became a British citizen. His later masterpiece, *Four Quartets*, is a meditation on time and faith. He also wrote the light verse that became the musical *Cats*. He won the Nobel Prize in 1948 and died in London on January 4, 1965.
