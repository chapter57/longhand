Charles Lamb was born in 1775 in the Inner Temple, the lawyers' quarter of London, where his father was a servant and clerk to a barrister. At Christ's Hospital school he became lifelong friends with Samuel Taylor Coleridge. Lamb had a bad stammer, which kept him from the church scholarships that would have sent him to university, so at seventeen he became a clerk at East India House, the headquarters of the East India Company. He stayed there for thirty-three years.

In September 1796, when Charles was twenty-one, his older sister Mary, in a sudden fit of madness, stabbed their mother to death with a kitchen knife. She was found insane and was not sent to prison. Charles persuaded the authorities to release her into his care, and promised to look after her for the rest of her life. He kept that promise. Mary had recurring breakdowns, and when they came, brother and sister would walk together, weeping, to the asylum, Mary carrying her own straitjacket.

Between those times, they were devoted companions and collaborators. Together they wrote *Tales from Shakespeare* (1807), retelling the plays in prose for children; Charles took the tragedies and Mary the comedies. It has never gone out of print. Their home was a gathering place for writers; Coleridge, Wordsworth and William Hazlitt were regular guests at their weekly evenings of cards and talk.

Lamb's great work began in 1820, when he started writing essays for the *London Magazine* under the name Elia, borrowed from a former colleague. The *Essays of Elia* are personal, rambling, funny and sad: about old clerks and old books, chimney sweeps, card games, his childhood, imaginary children he never had ("Dream-Children"), and the supposed Chinese discovery of roast pork, when a house burned down with a pig inside. He wrote them in a deliberately old-fashioned style, full of quirks, and they became loved for their warmth and honesty.

Lamb retired in 1825 with a pension. In the summer of 1834 Coleridge died, and Lamb never quite recovered from the loss. In December he fell on a walk, the wound became infected, and he died on December 27. Mary outlived him by thirteen years.
