Anton Pavlovich Chekhov was born in Taganrog, a port on the Sea of Azov in southern Russia. His grandfather had been a serf who bought his family's freedom; his father, a grocer, was a pious, violent man who beat his children. When the business failed, the family fled to Moscow to escape debts, leaving sixteen-year-old Anton behind to finish school and support himself by tutoring.

In Moscow he studied medicine, and to pay for his studies and keep his family afloat, he wrote short comic sketches for humor magazines under a string of pen names, hundreds of them, sometimes several a week. He qualified as a doctor in 1884. Medicine, he joked, was his lawful wife and literature his mistress.

Gradually the sketches grew deeper. Chekhov wrote stories about clerks, peasants, teachers, landowners and doctors, often without a dramatic plot or a moral, ending not with a resolution but with a character seeing their life a little more clearly. "The Lady with the Dog," "Gooseberries," "Ward No. 6" and "The Student" changed the short story forever. In 1890, already ill with tuberculosis, he crossed Siberia to the prison island of Sakhalin and conducted a census of its convicts, a book that helped bring reforms.

His plays were slower to find their audience. *The Seagull* was a disaster at its premiere in 1896, then a triumph two years later at the new Moscow Art Theatre, which went on to stage *Uncle Vanya*, *Three Sisters* and *The Cherry Orchard*. He married the actress Olga Knipper. In July 1904, at a German spa, he told the doctor in German that he was dying, drank a glass of champagne, and died. He was forty-four.
