Eric Arthur Blair was born in 1903 in Motihari, in India, where his father worked for the government's opium department, and was brought to England as a baby. He won a scholarship to Eton, and then, instead of going to university, joined the Indian Imperial Police in Burma. Five years there left him ashamed of empire, and he resigned to become a writer.

He chose a new name, George Orwell, and went looking for the lives most writers never saw. He washed dishes in Paris and slept in London's shelters for *Down and Out in Paris and London* (1933); he stayed with coal miners in the north of England for *The Road to Wigan Pier* (1937). In Spain he fought against Franco in a left-wing militia and was shot through the throat by a sniper; *Homage to Catalonia* (1938) told of the war, and of how Soviet-backed communists hunted down their own allies. It made him a lifelong enemy of totalitarianism of every kind.

He was above all an essayist. In "Shooting an Elephant," "Such, Such Were the Joys" and "Politics and the English Language" (1946), he argued that bad writing and bad politics feed each other: that vague, inflated language is how lies are made to sound truthful. He urged writers to cut every word they could and never use a long word where a short one will do.

*Animal Farm* (1945), a fable of farm animals whose revolution is betrayed by the pigs, made him famous. Already ill with tuberculosis, he wrote *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949) on the remote Scottish island of Jura. He died in a London hospital on January 21, 1950, at forty-six. Words he coined, "Big Brother," "doublethink," "thought police," have never been more in use.
