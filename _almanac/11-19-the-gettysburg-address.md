In early July 1863, the armies of the Union and the Confederacy fought for three days around the small town of Gettysburg, Pennsylvania. It was the bloodiest battle of the Civil War, and the dead lay in shallow graves across the fields. That autumn, a new cemetery was laid out for the Union soldiers, and November 19 was set for its dedication.

The main speaker was Edward Everett, a former senator, governor and president of Harvard, and the most celebrated orator of the day. He spoke for about two hours, describing the battle in detail. President Lincoln had been invited, almost as an afterthought, to offer "a few appropriate remarks."

The popular story that Lincoln scribbled his speech on the back of an envelope on the train is not true. He drafted it in Washington and revised it in the house where he stayed in Gettysburg the night before. When his turn came, he spoke fewer than three hundred words, and it took about two minutes. Some in the crowd hardly realized he had begun before he was done.

In those ten sentences, Lincoln did something remarkable. He began with the founding of the nation, "four score and seven years ago," in 1776, rather than with the Constitution, and so made the Declaration of Independence, with its claim that all men are created equal, the country's true starting point. He turned a cemetery dedication into a statement of what the war was for: that the nation might have "a new birth of freedom," and that "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."

He also predicted, wrongly, that "the world will little note, nor long remember what we say here." Everett knew better. The next day he wrote to Lincoln: "I should be glad, if I could flatter myself that I came as near to the central idea of the occasion, in two hours, as you did in two minutes."

Five handwritten copies of the address survive, each a little different. The words are carved in stone on the wall of the Lincoln Memorial in Washington.
