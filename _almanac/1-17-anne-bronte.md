Anne Brontë was born in Thornton, Yorkshire, the youngest of six children of an Irish clergyman. Before she was two, the family moved to the parsonage at Haworth, on the edge of the moors, and soon after her mother died. Two older sisters died in childhood; the surviving four, Charlotte, Branwell, Emily and Anne, grew up inventing imaginary kingdoms and writing tiny books about them.

Anne was the quiet one, and the one who went out into the world. She worked for years as a governess, an unhappy and lonely job, and later with her brother Branwell for a wealthy family at Thorp Green, where he disgraced himself, and both were dismissed.

Her first novel, *Agnes Grey* (1847), drew on her life as a governess: a clear-eyed, unsentimental account of how badly the rich treated the young women who taught their children. It was published, under the name Acton Bell, alongside Emily's *Wuthering Heights*. Her second, *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* (1848), was bolder. Its heroine, Helen, flees her violent, alcoholic husband with their small son, at a time when a married woman had no legal right to her own child or her own earnings, and supports herself as a painter. It sold out quickly and scandalized reviewers. Anne answered them in a preface: a good book is good whatever the sex of its author.

She died of tuberculosis in 1849, at twenty-nine, in the seaside town of Scarborough, and is the only Brontë buried away from Haworth. After her death, Charlotte kept *Tenant* from being reprinted, and for a century Anne was overshadowed by her sisters. Many readers now think her the boldest of the three.
