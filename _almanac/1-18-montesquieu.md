Charles-Louis de Secondat was born at the Château de La Brède, near Bordeaux, into a family of lawyers and minor nobles. At his baptism a beggar was chosen as his godfather, so that he would always remember the poor. He studied law, and from an uncle inherited both a fortune and the title Baron de Montesquieu, along with a seat as a judge in the high court of Bordeaux.

He made his name in 1721 with *Persian Letters*, published anonymously. It is a novel in letters, written by two Persian noblemen, Usbek and Rica, traveling in France and describing what they see to friends back home. Through their puzzled, outsiders' eyes, Paris looks absurd: the king, the pope, fashion, the salons, the Church. Meanwhile, back in Persia, Usbek's harem slowly rebels against him. It was a bestseller and a model for a century of satire. One of its most famous lines turns the question around: how can anyone be Persian?

He sold his judgeship, traveled across Europe, lived for two years in England, and spent twenty years on *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), a vast study of how governments work and how laws grow out of climate, religion, trade and custom. Its most influential idea was that liberty is safest when the powers of government, to make laws, to carry them out and to judge, are held by separate bodies that check each other. The authors of the American Constitution cited him more than almost any other writer.

The Church put both books on its index of forbidden works. Montesquieu, nearly blind at the end, died in Paris in 1755.
