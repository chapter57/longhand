Simone de Beauvoir was born in Paris into a respectable Catholic family that lost its money after the First World War. Her father told her she had a man's brain, and that she would have to work for a living, since there was no dowry. She lost her faith as a teenager and decided to become a philosopher.

In 1929, studying for France's most competitive teaching examination in philosophy, she met Jean-Paul Sartre, who came first that year; she came second, at twenty-one, among the youngest ever to pass. They became partners for the rest of their lives, without marrying or living together, reading each other's every manuscript, at the café tables of Saint-Germain-des-Prés.

She taught philosophy, then wrote full time. Her novels include *She Came to Stay* and *The Mandarins*, which won the Prix Goncourt, France's top literary prize, in 1954. Her memoirs, beginning with *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter*, are among the great autobiographies of the century.

But her most influential book is *The Second Sex* (1949), a vast study of how women have been defined, by biology, history, myth and literature, as the "other" to men. Its most famous idea, that a woman is not born but becomes one, shaped by a world that makes her, became a founding principle of modern feminism. The Vatican put the book on its list of forbidden works. Beauvoir died in Paris in 1986 and was buried beside Sartre in the Montparnasse cemetery.
