Thomas Gray was born in Cornhill, in the City of London, the fifth of twelve children and the only one to survive infancy. His father was violent; his mother left him for a time and ran a milliner's shop with her sister, and it was her earnings that paid for Thomas to go to Eton. There he made close friends with Horace Walpole, the son of the prime minister, and Richard West, a gifted young poet.

He spent most of his life at Cambridge as a scholar, living quietly in his college rooms, reading enormously in many languages, studying botany and old Norse and Welsh poetry, and writing very little. He was shy, fastidious and prone to melancholy. When Richard West died young in 1742, Gray was heartbroken, and wrote a sonnet in his memory.

That same year, it seems, he began a poem set in a country churchyard at evening, very likely at Stoke Poges in Buckinghamshire, where his mother lived. It took him years to finish. The speaker, as dusk falls, looks at the plain graves of villagers and thinks about the lives they lived: poor, obscure, unrecorded. Perhaps some of them had gifts that never found a chance to flower. "Full many a flower is born to blush unseen, / And waste its sweetness on the desert air."

When Walpole began passing the finished poem around, it was copied and spread so widely that Gray rushed it into print in 1751, to stop a magazine from publishing a pirated version. The *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard* became one of the most popular poems in English. Many of its lines are still part of the language: "the short and simple annals of the poor," "the paths of glory lead but to the grave," and "far from the madding crowd," which Thomas Hardy borrowed for a novel.

There is a famous story that General James Wolfe, the night before his attack on Quebec in 1759, recited the poem to his officers and said he would rather have written it than take the city. In 1757 Gray was offered the post of Poet Laureate. He turned it down. He died in Cambridge in 1771 and was buried at Stoke Poges, in the churchyard of his poem.
