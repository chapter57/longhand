Friedrich Schiller was born in Marbach, a small town in the German duchy of Württemberg. He wanted to study for the church, but the duke, who ran his state like a barracks, ordered him into his new military academy. Schiller studied law, then medicine, and became a regimental doctor. All the while he was secretly writing a play.

*The Robbers* is the story of a nobleman's son who, cheated of his inheritance by his scheming brother, becomes the leader of a band of outlaws in the forest. It opened in Mannheim in January 1782, and the audience went wild. One witness described strangers falling into each other's arms in tears. Schiller had slipped across the border without leave to attend. When he did it again, the duke had him arrested and forbade him to write anything but medical works. That September, Schiller fled.

The next years were hard and hand-to-mouth. In 1785 he went to stay with a new friend and admirer, Christian Gottfried Körner, near Leipzig and in Dresden, and in that happy time he wrote "An die Freude," the "Ode to Joy," a poem celebrating friendship and the brotherhood of all people.

Schiller became a professor of history at Jena and wrote essays on beauty and freedom that are still read by philosophers. Then, in 1794, he began a friendship with Goethe, ten years older and already Germany's most famous writer. They met almost daily, wrote to each other constantly, edited each other's work and challenged each other to write more, and in the last decade of Schiller's life, living near Goethe in Weimar, he wrote his greatest plays: the *Wallenstein* trilogy, *Mary Stuart*, *The Maid of Orleans* and *William Tell*. He was often ill, and he died in 1805, at forty-five.

In 1824, Beethoven, almost totally deaf, finished his Ninth Symphony with a setting for chorus of part of Schiller's "Ode to Joy," something almost unheard of in a symphony. The tune, without the words, is now the anthem of the European Union.
