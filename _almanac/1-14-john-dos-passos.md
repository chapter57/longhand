John Roderigo Dos Passos was born in a Chicago hotel, the son of a wealthy lawyer of Portuguese descent and a mother who was not, at the time, his wife; his parents married only when he was a teenager. He grew up mostly in Europe, attended Harvard, and in the First World War drove an ambulance in France and Italy.

The war turned him into a radical, and a writer. *Three Soldiers* (1921) was one of the first American novels to show the war without glory. *Manhattan Transfer* (1925) broke New York City into dozens of interlocking lives, cutting between them the way a film cuts between scenes.

His great work is the *U.S.A.* trilogy (1930–36), an attempt to portray the whole of American life in the first decades of the century. Alongside its fictional characters, it uses three experimental devices: "Newsreels," collages of real headlines and song lyrics; "Camera Eye" sections, stream-of-consciousness passages drawn from his own life; and short, sharp biographies of real Americans, from Henry Ford to the dancer Isadora Duncan. Jean-Paul Sartre called him the greatest writer of their time.

Disillusioned by the Spanish Civil War, where he saw a friend killed, he turned away from the left and in later life became a conservative, which cost him many of his old admirers. He died in Baltimore in 1970. *U.S.A.* is still one of the most ambitious American novels ever attempted.
