Samuel Johnson was born in 1709 in Lichfield, in the English Midlands, the son of a struggling bookseller. As a baby he caught scrofula, a form of tuberculosis, which scarred his face and damaged his sight and hearing. He grew up huge, awkward and twitching with tics, and astonishingly well read, having devoured his father's stock. He went to Oxford but had to leave after a year when the money ran out.

He came to London in 1737 with a former pupil, the actor David Garrick, and spent years scraping a living as a hack writer, producing whatever booksellers would pay for. In 1746 a group of them commissioned him to write a dictionary of the English language. The French Academy had spent decades on its dictionary with forty scholars; Johnson promised to do it in three years, with a few clerks to help him copy.

It took nine. The *Dictionary*, published in 1755, defined some 42,000 words and illustrated them with around 114,000 quotations from English writers, which Johnson had marked in his own reading. It was the standard English dictionary for more than a century. Some definitions show his sense of humor. "Oats" is "a grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people." "Lexicographer": "a writer of dictionaries; a harmless drudge."

He wrote much else: essays in *The Rambler* and *The Idler*, a famous edition of Shakespeare, *The Lives of the Poets*, and *Rasselas*, a short philosophical tale he wrote in the evenings of a single week to pay for his mother's funeral. He was a great talker, and his friend James Boswell, who wrote down that talk, made him one of the best-known people in English history.

He was also a writer of hard common sense about writing. "No man but a blockhead ever wrote, except for money," he said. He suffered all his life from depression and from fear of death, and faced both with courage. He died in London on December 13, 1784, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
