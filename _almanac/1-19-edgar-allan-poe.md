Edgar Poe was born in Boston to two traveling actors. His father left the family, and his mother died in Richmond, Virginia, before he was three. He was taken in, though never formally adopted, by a Richmond merchant, John Allan, whose name he took. They quarreled bitterly over money and Poe's gambling debts, and Allan eventually cut him off.

Poe tried the army and West Point, then made his living, barely, as a magazine editor and critic in Richmond, Philadelphia and New York. He married his young cousin Virginia Clemm. He was a sharp, sometimes savage reviewer, and he thought hard about how literature works: a story, he argued, should be short enough to read at one sitting, and every word in it should serve a single effect.

He followed his own rules. His tales of terror, "The Fall of the House of Usher," "The Tell-Tale Heart," "The Pit and the Pendulum," are tightly built machines for dread. With "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841) and its brilliant amateur detective, C. Auguste Dupin, he invented the detective story; Sherlock Holmes owes him a great deal. "The Raven" (1845) made him famous overnight, though it earned him almost nothing. He later wrote an essay explaining, perhaps not entirely truthfully, exactly how he had built it.

Virginia died of tuberculosis in 1847. Two years later Poe was found in Baltimore, delirious and in someone else's clothes, and died a few days later, at forty. No one has ever explained what happened to him.
