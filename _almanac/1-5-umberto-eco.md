Umberto Eco was born in Alessandria, in northern Italy, and wrote his university thesis on the aesthetics of Thomas Aquinas. He went to work for Italian television, then became a professor at the University of Bologna, where he taught semiotics, the study of signs: how words, pictures, gestures, comic strips, advertisements and nearly anything else carry meaning.

He wrote learned books on medieval philosophy and on how readers make sense of texts, and also cheerful, sharp newspaper essays on James Bond, Superman, football and the absurdities of modern life. He was one of the first serious scholars to take popular culture seriously.

At forty-eight he published his first novel. *The Name of the Rose* (1980) is a murder mystery set in an Italian abbey in 1327. A Franciscan friar, William of Baskerville, and his young assistant investigate a series of deaths among the monks, and the clues lead to the abbey's great library, a labyrinth that holds a forbidden book. It is a detective story, a history of medieval arguments about poverty and laughter, and a book about books. It sold tens of millions of copies.

He wrote six more novels, including *Foucault's Pendulum*, about three editors who invent a conspiracy theory as a joke and watch it come true. He owned some fifty thousand books and liked to say that a library is not a collection of books you have read but of the ones you haven't, a reminder of everything you do not yet know. He died in Milan in 2016.
