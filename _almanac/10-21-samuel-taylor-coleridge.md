Samuel Taylor Coleridge was the youngest of a vicar's many children, born in Ottery St Mary in Devon. He was sent to school in London at Christ's Hospital, where he became lifelong friends with Charles Lamb, and then to Cambridge. There, deep in debt and unhappy in love, he ran off and enlisted in the cavalry under the absurd name of Silas Tomkyn Comberbache. He was a terrible horseman, and his brothers bought him out a few months later.

He and the poet Robert Southey once planned to start an ideal community on the banks of the Susquehanna River in Pennsylvania. That never happened, but in 1797, living in the village of Nether Stowey in Somerset, Coleridge became close friends with William Wordsworth, who lived a few miles away. For a year or so the two men walked and talked almost every day, and out of that came *Lyrical Ballads* (1798), published without their names. It opened with Coleridge's "Rime of the Ancient Mariner" and closed with Wordsworth's "Tintern Abbey," and it is often said to mark the beginning of English Romantic poetry.

Coleridge's most famous poem has the most famous excuse. He said that in 1797, after taking opium for an illness, he fell asleep over a travel book and composed two or three hundred lines of "Kubla Khan" in his sleep. On waking he began to write them down, but was called away by "a person on business from Porlock," and when he came back an hour later the rest had vanished. Readers have argued ever since about how much of that story to believe.

His addiction to opium shadowed much of his life, but he never stopped thinking. He gave celebrated lectures on Shakespeare, and his *Biographia Literaria* (1817) gave us a phrase every reader knows: "that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith."

For his last eighteen years he lived in the house of a doctor, James Gillman, in Highgate, north London. Young writers came to hear him talk, often for hours. Thomas Carlyle called him a sage. Keats met him once on a walk near Hampstead Heath, and wrote afterward that in two miles Coleridge "broached a thousand things."
