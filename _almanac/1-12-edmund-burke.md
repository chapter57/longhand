Edmund Burke was born in Dublin, the son of a Protestant lawyer and a Catholic mother, and studied at Trinity College there. He went to London to train for the law, gave it up, and became a writer.

His first important book, written in his twenties, was *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757). It asked why some things please us and others overwhelm us. Beauty, he argued, is small, smooth and gentle, and makes us feel love; the sublime is vast, dark, powerful and obscure, like storms, mountains and the ocean, and fills us with a kind of delightful terror. The distinction became essential to the Romantic poets and painters who followed, to Wordsworth, Shelley and Turner, and to the Gothic novel.

Then he went into politics. For nearly thirty years he sat in the House of Commons, one of the greatest speakers of his age. He argued for conciliation with the American colonies, attacked the corruption of the East India Company, and spent years prosecuting its governor of India, Warren Hastings.

In 1790 he published *Reflections on the Revolution in France*, warning that a revolution which tore up a society's traditions and institutions in the name of abstract reason would end in terror and tyranny, before the Terror had happened. It provoked furious replies, including Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man* and Mary Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Men*, and it is still argued over today. Burke died in 1797.
