Zora Neale Hurston was born in Notasulga, Alabama, and grew up in Eatonville, Florida, one of the first towns in America incorporated by Black citizens, where her father was a preacher and later mayor. The porch of the town store, where people traded stories, was her first school of literature. Her mother died when she was thirteen, and for years she drifted, working as a maid, before she talked her way back into high school at twenty-six by giving her age as sixteen.

She studied at Howard University and then at Barnard College, where she was the only Black student, and became a student of the anthropologist Franz Boas. With his encouragement she went back South with a notebook and, later, a pistol, collecting folktales, songs and sermons in Florida, Louisiana, Haiti and Jamaica. *Mules and Men* (1935) gathered them, and it is still a treasure of American folklore.

In Harlem in the 1920s she was one of the liveliest figures of the Harlem Renaissance. In a 1928 essay, "How It Feels to Be Colored Me," she wrote, with characteristic nerve, that she was "not tragically colored." Her masterpiece, the novel *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), follows Janie Crawford through three marriages in search of a love that lets her be herself. Hurston wrote it in seven weeks, in Haiti.

Her later years were hard. Her books went out of print, and she worked as a maid and a substitute teacher, and died in poverty in Florida in 1960. She was buried in an unmarked grave. In 1973 the novelist Alice Walker went looking for it, found the spot in an overgrown cemetery, and put up a stone: "A Genius of the South." Today Hurston is one of the most read American writers of the century.
