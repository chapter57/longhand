Marie-Henri Beyle was born in Grenoble, in the French Alps. His mother died when he was seven, and he hated his father, his town and the priest who tutored him with equal passion. At sixteen he went to Paris, and through a cousin found a place in Napoleon's army. He followed it into Italy, a country he loved for the rest of his life, and later to Moscow in 1812, where he survived the terrible retreat.

After Napoleon's fall he lived in Milan, writing books on music, painting and love, and published them under some two hundred pen names. The one that stuck was Stendhal. In Florence, he wrote, he was so overcome by the beauty of the church of Santa Croce that his heart pounded and he nearly fainted; such a reaction to art is still called "Stendhal syndrome."

*The Red and the Black* (1830) follows Julien Sorel, a clever, ambitious carpenter's son who, in the gray France after Napoleon, can rise only through the Church, the red of the army having closed to him, and through love affairs he plans like military campaigns. *The Charterhouse of Parma* (1839), which he dictated in about seven weeks, opens with a young Italian nobleman wandering bewildered through the Battle of Waterloo, never quite sure he's in it. Tolstoy said it taught him how to write about war.

Neither novel sold well. Stendhal, who spent his last years as French consul in a small papal port, said he expected to be understood around 1880. He collapsed in a Paris street in 1842 and died the next day. By the 1880s he had become, as he predicted, one of the great French novelists.
