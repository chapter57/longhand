John Robinson Jeffers was born in Pittsburgh, the son of a Presbyterian minister and professor who drilled him in Greek and Latin from early childhood. He was educated partly in Europe, studied medicine and forestry, and then, with a small inheritance, decided to be a poet.

In 1914 he and his wife Una moved to Carmel, then a tiny village on the wild coast south of San Francisco. On a granite headland above the Pacific, Jeffers built a stone cottage, hauling boulders up from the shore himself, and then a forty-foot tower, Hawk Tower, for Una. He worked on the stones in the afternoons and wrote in the mornings, and he lived there for the rest of his life. The house still stands.

His poems are unlike anyone else's: long, loose lines like waves, about rock, ocean, hawks and storms, and about the violence and vanity of human beings against the vast indifference of nature. He called his view "inhumanism": the idea that people should turn their attention outward, to the beauty of the world, rather than inward to themselves. His long narrative poems, such as *Tamar* and *Roan Stallion*, retold Greek tragedies on the California coast and made him famous in the 1920s.

His fame faded when he opposed America's entry into the Second World War, and his publisher printed a note disowning some of his poems. He died at Tor House in 1962. In recent decades, as readers have come to care about the natural world in new ways, his reputation has risen again.
