Gibran Khalil Gibran was born in Bsharri, a village in the mountains of what is now Lebanon, then part of the Ottoman Empire. His family was poor, and when he was twelve his mother took him and his siblings to Boston, where they lived in the crowded South End. There he was noticed by a teacher and then by a photographer and publisher, Fred Holland Day, who encouraged his drawing.

He went back to Beirut to study Arabic literature, returned to Boston, and lost his mother, his brother and a sister to illness within about a year. He began writing in Arabic, poems and stories critical of the church and of arranged marriages, and became a leading voice of a new movement in Arabic literature. He studied art in Paris and in 1911 settled in New York, in a studio in Greenwich Village.

His patron and closest friend was a Boston headmistress, Mary Haskell, who supported him for years and helped edit his English. *The Prophet* (1923) is a short book in which a wise man named Almustafa, about to sail home after twelve years in a foreign city, is asked by its people to speak to them of love, marriage, children, work, freedom, reason and passion, pain, friendship and death. Each answer is a page or two of rhythmic prose, illustrated with Gibran's own drawings.

It sold slowly at first, then never stopped. It has been translated into more than a hundred languages and read at countless weddings and funerals. Gibran died in New York in 1931, at forty-eight, and was buried in Lebanon, in a monastery near the village where he was born.
