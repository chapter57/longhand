Robert Bridges was a doctor for most of the years when other poets are making their names. Born in Walmer, Kent, he studied at Eton and at Oxford, then trained in medicine at St Bartholomew's Hospital in London. He practiced as a physician until 1882, when a serious illness made him stop, and he moved to the country to write. He had already decided, it is said, to give up medicine at forty for poetry; illness simply made it happen a little sooner.

His own poems are quiet, careful and musical, and he was fascinated by the sounds and rhythms of English verse. He helped compile a hymnbook, translated hymns, and co-founded the Society for Pure English to argue about how the language was spoken and spelled. In 1913 he was made Poet Laureate, a post he held until his death in 1930, and in his eighties he wrote a long philosophical poem, *The Testament of Beauty* (1929), which became an unexpected bestseller.

But he may be best remembered for another poet. At Oxford, Bridges became friends with Gerard Manley Hopkins, a brilliant and intense student who later became a Jesuit priest. For twenty years the two men wrote to each other, and Hopkins sent Bridges his poems: strange, dense, sprung with new rhythms, like nothing else being written in English. Almost none of them were published in Hopkins's lifetime.

When Hopkins died in Dublin in 1889, Bridges had the manuscripts. He waited almost thirty years before he published them, in 1918. People have argued about why he waited so long. Bridges himself thought the world was not ready, and in his introduction he gently scolded some of his friend's oddities. But he kept the poems safe and he got them into print, and when they appeared they astonished a new generation. "The Windhover," "Pied Beauty" and "God's Grandeur" exist for us because Robert Bridges kept faith with his friend.
