Lope de Vega was born in Madrid, the son of an embroiderer, and was a child prodigy who, it was said, was reading Latin and writing verse by the time he was five. He lived his whole life at full speed. He studied at university in Alcalá, sailed with the Spanish Armada against England in 1588, was banished from Madrid for writing libels against a former lover's family, married twice, had a long string of love affairs and many children, and in his fifties became a priest, which did not stop the love affairs.

Above all he wrote. He wrote sonnets, epics, novels and devotional verse, and he wrote for the theatre at a pace no one has matched. He claimed to have written some fifteen hundred plays; his first biographer put the number higher. Around four hundred survive. He could reportedly finish a full-length play in a day. Miguel de Cervantes, his great rival, called him a "monster of nature," meaning a marvel. In Madrid, people praised anything excellent by saying it was "de Lope," Lope's.

Lope did more than anyone to create the Spanish theatre of the Golden Age. In a short verse essay, *The New Art of Writing Plays* (1609), he cheerfully admitted that he ignored the classical rules, because the paying audience wanted something else: three acts, mixed tragedy and comedy, honor, disguises, love and swordfights, and plenty of action. His plays include comedies like *The Dog in the Manger* and the dark, poetic *The Knight from Olmedo*.

His best-known play today is *Fuenteovejuna*. It is based on a real event of 1476, when the people of a village in Andalusia rose up and killed their lord, a military commander who had abused them. When a royal judge arrives and tortures the villagers one by one to find out who did it, each answers only, "Fuenteovejuna did it." The king, unable to punish a whole town, pardons them. It is one of the first great plays about the collective resistance of ordinary people, and it has been staged around the world, often at moments of political upheaval.

When Lope died in 1635, the streets of Madrid were crowded for his funeral, which lasted nine days.
