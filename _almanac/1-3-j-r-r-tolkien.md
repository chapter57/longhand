John Ronald Reuel Tolkien was born in Bloemfontein, in South Africa, where his father managed a bank. After his father's death the family settled near Birmingham, in England, and when he was twelve his mother died too, leaving him and his brother in the care of a Catholic priest. As a schoolboy he fell in love with languages, and began inventing his own.

He fought in the Battle of the Somme in 1916 and lost most of his closest friends in the war. While recovering from trench fever, he began writing the myths and histories of an imagined world for his invented languages to belong to. He spent the rest of his life adding to it.

His day job was philology, the study of old languages. He was a professor of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford, and in 1936 he gave a lecture, "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics," arguing that the dragon and the monsters were not embarrassing fairy-tale additions to a historical poem, as scholars had thought, but the heart of it. It changed how the poem is read.

He began *The Hobbit* by writing its first line on the blank page of an exam he was marking. It came out in 1937, and his publisher asked for a sequel. Seventeen years later, it arrived: *The Lord of the Rings* (1954–55), so long that it was published in three volumes. He met regularly with C. S. Lewis and a group of friends called the Inklings, who read their work aloud to each other in an Oxford pub. Tolkien died in 1973. His readers number in the hundreds of millions, and he would have liked best those who went on to learn some Old English.
