Francis Bacon was born in London, the son of Queen Elizabeth's Lord Keeper of the Great Seal, and went to Cambridge at twelve. He trained as a lawyer, entered Parliament at twenty-three, and spent decades climbing the ladder of royal favor. Under James I he rose to the top: Attorney General, and in 1618 Lord Chancellor, the highest legal office in England.

Alongside his career he wrote. In 1597 he published a slim book of ten short *Essays*, borrowing the form and the name from Montaigne. Over his life he expanded it to fifty-eight. Where Montaigne's essays wander and confide, Bacon's are compressed, practical and worldly: "Of Truth," "Of Death," "Of Revenge," "Of Marriage and Single Life," "Of Studies." Nearly every sentence is quotable, and many have become proverbs. They are the first great essays in English.

He also wanted to rebuild all of human knowledge. In *The Advancement of Learning* and *Novum Organum* (1620), he argued that natural philosophy should rest not on ancient authorities but on careful observation and experiment, gathering facts and reasoning upward from them. He is called a father of the scientific method, and the Royal Society, founded after his death, took him as its inspiration. *New Atlantis* imagined a scientific utopia.

In 1621 he was accused of taking bribes as a judge. He admitted it, was fined and briefly imprisoned in the Tower, and was barred from office. He spent his last years writing. He died in 1626, and a famous story, told by the gossip John Aubrey, says he caught his death experimenting on a snowy day, stuffing a chicken with snow to see whether cold would preserve meat.
