Barbara Wertheim was born in New York into a wealthy family with a strong sense of public life. Her grandfather Henry Morgenthau Sr. was the American ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, and her uncle would become Franklin Roosevelt's Treasury Secretary. At two, she was on board a ship in the Mediterranean when the first shots of the First World War were fired at sea nearby, an episode she later wrote about.

She graduated from Radcliffe College and never went to graduate school. Instead, she became a journalist, reporting from Spain during its civil war, and later married a doctor, Lester Tuchman, and raised three daughters while writing history in her spare hours. Some academic historians looked down on her; she said that having no doctorate was what saved her, because no one had taught her to write badly.

*The Guns of August* (1962) told how the great powers of Europe stumbled into the First World War in its first month, through pride, miscalculation and rigid plans. It was a bestseller and won the Pulitzer Prize. President Kennedy read it, and during the Cuban Missile Crisis that October, he is said to have recalled its lesson about leaders blundering into a war nobody wanted. She won a second Pulitzer for *Stilwell and the American Experience in China*.

*A Distant Mirror* (1978) brought the calamitous fourteenth century, the age of the Black Death, to life through the career of a single French knight. Tuchman believed history should be told as a story, with real people, vivid detail and suspense, and that writing it well was not decoration but the point. She died in 1989.
