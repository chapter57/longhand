Mary Elizabeth Mapes was born in New York City, the daughter of a scientist and inventor, and was educated at home by tutors in a house full of visitors from the city's literary and scientific worlds. At twenty she married a lawyer, William Dodge, and had two sons. In 1858 her husband disappeared and was later found drowned. Left with two small boys and little money, she began to write.

*Hans Brinker, or the Silver Skates* (1865) is the story of a poor Dutch boy and his sister, who dream of winning a pair of silver skates in a race on the frozen canals, while their father lies ill from a terrible injury. Dodge had never been to the Netherlands; she wrote it from books and from stories told by Dutch neighbors. The book was a sensation, and gave many Americans their picture of Holland, including the famous story of the little boy who saves his town by putting his finger in a leaking dike, which is told in the book as a tale the characters read.

In 1873 she became the founding editor of *St. Nicholas*, a magazine for children, and for over thirty years she made it the best of its kind. She believed children deserved real literature, not moral lectures, and she asked the finest writers to write for them. Mark Twain, Louisa May Alcott, Rudyard Kipling, Frances Hodgson Burnett, Robert Louis Stevenson and many others appeared in its pages; *Little Lord Fauntleroy* and some of Kipling's jungle stories first came out there. The young readers of *St. Nicholas* included future writers like F. Scott Fitzgerald and Edna St. Vincent Millay.

Dodge died in 1905 at her summer cottage in the Catskills. She had, more than anyone, invented the idea of literature for children.
