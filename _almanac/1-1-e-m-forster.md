Edward Morgan Forster was born in London on New Year's Day, 1879. His father, an architect, died before the boy was two, and he was raised by his mother and a household of devoted aunts, which may explain his lifelong ear for the polite tyrannies of English family life. A legacy from a great-aunt left him enough money to be free to write.

In six years he published four novels about the English middle classes and what they suppress: *Where Angels Fear to Tread*, *The Longest Journey*, *A Room with a View* (1908), in which a young woman in Florence must choose between convention and feeling, and *Howards End* (1910), about two families and the house that comes between them. Its epigraph is two words long, and it has become a kind of motto: "Only connect."

Then, for fourteen years, he published no novel. He worked in Egypt for the Red Cross during the First World War and twice traveled to India, and in 1924 he published *A Passage to India*, about friendship between an Englishman and an Indian under British rule, and the strange event in the Marabar Caves that destroys it. It was his last novel, though he lived for nearly half a century more. He was gay, and a novel he wrote about a love between two men, *Maurice*, was published only after his death.

In 1927 he gave a series of lectures at Cambridge, published as *Aspects of the Novel*. They are conversational, funny and wise, and they gave readers terms they still use: "flat" and "round" characters, and the difference between a story and a plot. "The king died and then the queen died" is a story, he wrote. "The king died, and then the queen died of grief" is a plot. If you want one book to make you a better reader of novels, it is this one.
