Isaac Asimov was born in Petrovichi, in Russia, around 1920, and came to Brooklyn with his family when he was three. His parents ran a series of candy stores, and the boy, who worked behind the counter, discovered science fiction in the pulp magazines on the racks. He sold his first story at eighteen and went on to earn a doctorate in chemistry at Columbia.

In the 1940s he wrote the stories that made his name. The robot stories, collected as *I, Robot* (1950), introduced his Three Laws of Robotics, rules for machines that must not harm people, and the word "robotics" itself, which he coined. The Foundation stories imagined a mathematician who can predict the fall of a galactic empire and plans how to shorten the dark age that will follow. They are, among other things, a meditation on Gibbon's *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*.

But Asimov was, above all, an explainer. He became a professor of biochemistry at Boston University and then gave up teaching to write full time, which he did for most of every day, on a typewriter, for decades. He wrote guides to the Bible and to Shakespeare, histories, books of jokes and limericks, and hundreds of essays and books making science clear to ordinary readers. By the end he had written or edited around five hundred books, across nearly every section of the library.

He was afraid of flying and rarely traveled; he liked small, enclosed rooms and the sound of his own typewriter. He died in New York in 1992. He believed that knowledge was the cure for most of what is wrong with the world, and he spent his life trying to give it away.
