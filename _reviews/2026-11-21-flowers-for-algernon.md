A classic that has always managed to keep me reflecting upon its theme since I first read it over forty years ago is *Flowers for Algernon* by Daniel Keyes. He wrote it in the late fifties as a novella, and then later adapted it for film. Finally, he published the full novel in 1966. This is the version I picked up as a teenager in the early eighties.

Algernon is a lab mouse that underwent an experimental treatment to improve cognition. Charlie is a human selected to undergo the same experiment due to his cognitive impairment. He is a kind man with childlike interest and observations.

Spoilers ahead! The experiment is a success and readers experience joy as Charlie is rewarded with a newfound ability to learn. The learning never stops, and Charlie quickly becomes a genius capable of evaluating his transformation. His eyes open, he grows increasingly depressed with what he now knows of the world. He is especially hurt by the realization of how people perceived him for most of his life. Before it all began, Charlie had little self-awareness and his daily interactions were not a source of pain. The book is an in depth illustration of the adage, ignorance is bliss.

It is true. It occurs to me all the time. With the internet, any curiosity can be satisfied. The news of the world and the hardships are always just a keystroke away. My curiosity causes me to doomscroll upon waking every morning. I can clearly see all the problems. I might know what needs to happen to reduce harm, but I am still helpless to change almost all of what I see. The more I understand, the sadder I become. I can never forget Charlie because I have been Charlie. I am still Charlie as he begins his regression.

I am experiencing cognitive decline from both natural aging and certain health conditions. It has been gradually getting worse. I can no longer retain new information or memorize poems as I did when I was younger. Some days I can't recall a term that I know and use often. I have to tell Alexa everything I need to do so I can remember to do it. I need lists for everything and I am easily sidetracked. I can take pills, then have no idea whether I took pills. If I am suspicious, I can decide not to take more. I think more often I take them twice because I was not aware enough to suspect.

Over the last two years I have also noticed I have a rather blissful acceptance of this. I am content when I let my curiosity go. I am still aware enough to realize that this is not normal. I am always thinking of Charly. I think, I am happy because I am regressing. Soon I won't be bothered by anything, and I will just forget to plan trips, or do my taxes, or eat. Who knows?

Perhaps every painful observation will just recede into a vague smile – just like it did for Charly.
